Radiation is one of the main challenges for people living and working outside Earth's atmosphere, so space agencies measure the dose each crew member receives.

Neutron radiation is about 30% of the total radiation on the International Space Station. Neutrons can cause serious biological damage and need special shielding, which is why neutron dose is measured separately.

The Canadian Space Agency flew bubble detectors and the Radi-N and Radi-N2 experiments on the station to measure neutrons, and is funding the Canadian Active Neutron Spectrometer (CANS), a compact instrument that measures neutron exposure continuously and on its own.

Continuous radiation readings help crews and flight surgeons lower health risks on Moon and Mars missions: check your personal dosimeter reading each day and go to the shelter when TRIX reports a solar storm.
